## What I heard

I attended part of the Covington Board of Commissioners caucus on August 18, 2026. I arrived
after the meeting had begun, so my recording and notes cover only the later portion. To make
the account more reliable, I compared what I heard with the City's official agenda packet and
the Police Department's written license-plate-reader policy.

Roughly, my recording covers:

| Approximate time | Subject |
| --- | --- |
| 00:00–02:43 | Public Works and Code Enforcement vehicle purchases; the public purchasing process |
| 02:44–04:20 | Fiscal-year 2027 Southbank Partners contribution |
| 04:22–06:29 | Introduction to the proposed Axon technology package |
| 06:30–07:13 | Competition, integration, vendor lock-in, and data migration |
| 07:14–09:32 | Flock comparison, Axon vehicle-mounted ALPR, access, sharing, retention, and Kentucky law |
| 09:40–14:30 | Storm response, interagency work, city employees, and residents helping neighbors |
| ~14:31 | Formal adjournment |

I was interested in the explanation of why purchases that are already budgeted still come back
to a public agenda. The budget establishes authority, but the public meeting gives commissioners
and residents another chance to see what's being bought and ask why it's needed.

The storm discussion gave the meeting a more human ending. Commissioners described city
employees, partner agencies, and residents clearing hazards, redirecting traffic, delivering
food, and checking on affected households. What stayed with me was the shift from immediate
emergency work to helping people whose groceries had spoiled during outages — a practical
picture of public service that doesn't stop when the road reopens.

The eight Public Works trucks total $455,429.72. The packet says the purchases use state-bid
pricing.

**Southbank Partners.** A fiscal-year 2027 membership contribution under an interlocal
partnership: $82,750, described as a one-time annual membership fee. Meeting speakers described
Southbank as a partnership among Northern Kentucky river cities and mentioned examples including
digital kiosks, regional riverfront or grant work, and a Latonia-focused assessment — I'm
treating those project descriptions as meeting statements until I can verify their exact names
independently.

**The Axon proposal.** Axon Enterprise Inc. proposed a 10-year agreement, expected to run from
October 1, 2026 through September 2036, at a total value of $6,025,083.04. It would replace a
five-year Axon agreement expiring September 30, 2026. The packet's payment schedule shows
$590,779.06 in September 2026, $117,292.80 in October 2026, and $590,779.02 each September from
2027 through 2035. The item advanced from caucus toward a legislative meeting — the paperwork
points to August 25, 2026 — and was **not** finally approved on August 18.

Components identified in the packet include 117 body-worn-camera bundles, 117 TASER 10 plans,
four Axon Fleet 3 systems, four vehicle-mounted ALPR licenses, interview-room equipment,
Evidence.com storage, auto-tagging and redaction tools, transcription, the Axon AI Assistant,
Draft One AI-assisted report writing, and an Axon Air/Skydio drone-as-first-responder component.

Commissioners asked whether other vendors could supply the same integrated system, whether using
multiple vendors would create incompatible systems, how difficult and expensive it would be to
leave Axon, how the proposed readers differ from Flock, who can search or receive ALPR data, and
how long ALPR data is retained. Staff said separating from Axon and migrating City-owned data
would take substantial time and additional funding — I'm attributing that as a statement made at
the meeting, not an independently calculated cost.

**Flock vs. Axon.** Covington did not choose Flock at this meeting. A commissioner raised Flock
as a comparison because it had been in the news. The proposal on the table was a 10-year Axon
package containing four vehicle-mounted Axon ALPR systems. The chief described the proposed Axon
readers as cruiser-mounted equipment that reads plates while officers travel, not a new network
of fixed cameras. But Covington's written policy says the Police Department uses **both**
fixed-location and vehicle-mounted ALPR systems — the fixed equipment is operated through a
partnership with the Housing Authority of Covington. So I'm not generalizing the chief's comment
into "Covington has no fixed ALPR cameras" — the accurate statement is that the four systems in
*this* Axon proposal are vehicle-mounted.

**ALPR safeguards.** Covington Police General Order 40.01, revised July 29, 2026, says ALPR use
is limited to trained operators for official law-enforcement purposes; anyone browsing detections
has to document the reason and, when available, a case or incident number; access requires
individual login credentials; officers must verify an alert matches the observed plate and
validate it before taking enforcement action; an unvalidated alert can't by itself justify
restricting someone's freedom; default retention is no more than 90 days, with stated exceptions;
detection-browsing inquiries are audited every 90 days, with the auditor randomly examining at
least 10 inquiries per ALPR system and reporting findings to the police chief; and the department
prohibits selling ALPR images, limiting sharing to what Kentucky law allows. The policy names
comparison databases including NCIC, the Law Information Network of Kentucky, law-enforcement
hot lists, provider/private-entity hot lists, and hot lists maintained by Covington's Community
Liaison and Intelligence Unit. Kentucky law has specific sharing exceptions — including to law
enforcement, contracted third parties, the National Insurance Crime Bureau, insurers, financial
institutions, and in response to subpoenas — so I'm not stating that no one outside Covington can
ever receive the data; the exceptions are real and worth naming honestly.

**Storm response.** Officials said the City had received 178 service calls and responded to 133
by that point in the meeting. Public Works crews cleared streets into the evening and continued
street sweeping the next morning. Residents improvised barriers and helped redirect traffic
around debris and downed power lines. The response expanded to food assistance after power
outages spoiled groceries, with food arriving from Frankfort, and fire, Code Enforcement, and
other personnel went door to door in affected areas. I'm attributing these figures and
observations to comments made at the caucus unless a later City report confirms them
independently.

## What stood out to me

What stood out to me was how much can sit behind a short agenda description. The proposed Axon
item was labeled as police equipment, software, and licensing, but the packet describes a
10-year, $6.025 million package involving body cameras, fleet cameras, TASER plans, evidence
storage, mobile license-plate readers, AI-assisted report writing, and a drone component. That's
not necessarily an argument for or against the agreement — it's a reason the public discussion
should match the full scope of the decision.

## My view

I was encouraged that commissioners asked about privacy and vendor lock-in. An integrated
platform may save time and reduce compatibility problems, but a system becomes harder to leave
the more cameras, evidence, weapons, reports, and daily workflows depend on it. My view is that
capability and accountability should grow together. If Covington enters a 10-year agreement,
residents should get clear performance information, meaningful audit summaries, understandable
data-sharing rules, and an exit plan that protects City-owned records. I'm not treating this
technology as automatically good or automatically harmful — I understand why an integrated
system can reduce administrative work and make evidence easier to manage. AI-assisted reports
and drone deployment, especially, deserve direct public discussion on their own terms, not just
inclusion inside a larger bundle.

## Questions I'm following

**Axon and ALPR** — Did the Board approve, amend, delay, or reject the agreement? What's the
final executed contract value? How many fixed and vehicle-mounted ALPR systems are actually
active? Which external networks and hot lists are enabled? Can outside agencies search Covington
data directly? How often is data retained beyond 90 days, and under which exception? Will the
City publish non-sensitive summaries of its 90-day audits? How many alerts are false or need
correction?

**AI-assisted reports** — Must officers verify every generated sentence against recordings and
notes? Is AI assistance disclosed in the case record? Are prompts, drafts, and edits retained?
What training and supervisory review are required?

**Drone program** — Which calls justify a launch, and who authorizes each flight? What
geographic, warrant, and privacy rules apply? How long is footage retained? Will the City publish
a flight log and aggregate statistics?

**Vehicles and Southbank** — Which older vehicles are being replaced, and what happens to them?
What savings or service improvements result? What Covington-specific projects, grants, or
outcomes has the Southbank membership actually produced?

**Storm response** — What final service-call and response totals did the City report? What did
the after-action review identify for improvement?

## What happens next

The Axon item was advanced from caucus, but it was not finally approved that evening. I'm
keeping the status as "advanced from caucus" and will update this entry after the Board takes
formal action — the paperwork points to a legislative meeting on August 25, 2026. I'll also keep
following the City's ALPR audits and the rules that end up governing AI-assisted reports and
drone deployment.
